Set expectations before anything else: this is a roundtable, not a lecture. I'll walk the Django process because it's concrete, public and stress-tested - but Django is the worked example, not the subject. Say explicitly that the interesting conversation is the one it opens onto: what does good open source contribution look like, and what do any of us actually owe a contributor showing up at our own repos? Invite people to interrupt with parallels from their own projects and teams - that tangent IS the session, not a derailment of it.

Two Things, Briefly

The general case first — then the example we’ll spend the session on.

What is open source?

📜

A licence

Anyone may use, read, modify and redistribute the code. The legal floor — and the least interesting part.

👁️

A process in public

Not just the source. The bug reports, the review comments, the arguments and the rejections are all visible too.

🤝

A community, not a vendor

Contributors arrive unbidden and unmanaged. Nobody can be assigned work — only persuaded to do it.

🚪

A way in

Which is the bit we’re here for: how does a stranger’s idea become code you ship?

And what is Django?

🐍

A Python web framework

Batteries included: ORM, migrations, admin, auth, templating, forms, i18n, security defaults — in the box, not bolted on.

📅

Public since 2005

Born at a newspaper — the Lawrence Journal-World in Kansas — and BSD licensed from the start.

🎸

Named after Django Reinhardt

The Belgian-Romani jazz guitarist (1910–1953), pioneer of gypsy jazz — who played his best-known work with two fretting fingers after a fire injury.

🧭

An opinionated philosophy

DRY, loose coupling, explicit over implicit, and less code — pragmatism over purity, written down publicly so decisions can be argued against.

Both rows are definitions the room mostly already has - go fast, don't teach. Top row: the move to make is that open source is not mainly a licensing fact. The licence is the floor. What actually distinguishes it is that the process is public and the labour is unassignable - you can persuade contributors, never task them. That constraint shapes every gate in the flow we're about to walk, and it's also the thing that DOESN'T hold inside our own companies. Worth planting that now; the room will pick at it later. Bottom row: now narrow to the example. The point is age and philosophy. Django is old enough that its process has been stress-tested, and opinionated enough that it has to decide what does and doesn't belong in core. The published design philosophy means "does this fit Django?" is a question the intake process can answer rather than a matter of taste.

So Who Actually Runs Django?

The licence is the easy bit. The governance is the interesting bit.

Governance structure & roles

  • Django Software Foundation (DSF) — the non-profit that holds the trademark and the money
  • Steering Council — elected; rules on direction and significant changes
  • Mergers — appointed by the Steering Council; the only ones who commit to main
  • Fellows — paid contributors who triage, review and keep the lights on
  • DSF members & the wider community — vote, file, patch and review

Protocols

  • DEPs — Django Enhancement Proposals, for the big stuff
  • Ticket spec — every Trac ticket carries a type, component and flags (has patch, needs docs, needs tests)
  • Triage stages — Unreviewed → Accepted → Ready for checkin
  • Deprecation policy — warn for two releases before removing
  • Security policy — private report, pre-notification, coordinated release

Forums

  • Django Forum — forum.djangoproject.com; discussion before decision
  • Discord — real-time chat and help for new contributors
  • new-features repo — feature ideas triaged by the Steering Council
  • Security email — the one private channel, for vulnerabilities

Codebases

  • Django — django/django, the framework itself
  • djangoproject.com — the website, itself a Django app

Services

  • GitHub — hosting, pull requests, Actions CI
  • Trac — the bug tracker (code.djangoproject.com)
  • Jenkins — the full test matrix (djangoci.com)
  • Transifex — community translations

Platforms

DSF-hosted

  • djangoproject.com
  • Trac
  • Django Forum
  • Jenkins

Third-party

  • GitHub
  • Discord
  • Transifex
The key point: "open source" here doesn't mean "anyone can push". It means the process is public. Note the Fellows - Django solved the maintainer-burnout problem by paying people to do the unglamorous work. Worth flagging that as a deliberate design decision, not an accident.

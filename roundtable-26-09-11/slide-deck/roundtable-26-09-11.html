Set expectations before anything else: this is a roundtable, not a lecture. I'll walk the Django process because it's concrete, public and stress-tested - but Django is the worked example, not the subject. Say explicitly that the interesting conversation is the one it opens onto: what does good open source contribution look like, and what do any of us actually owe a contributor showing up at our own repos? Invite people to interrupt with parallels from their own projects and teams - that tangent IS the session, not a derailment of it.
  • Open source — code anyone can use and change
  • Django — a batteries-included Python web framework
Both are definitions the room mostly already has - go fast, don't teach. Open source: the move to make is that open source is not mainly a licensing fact. The licence is the floor. What actually distinguishes it is that the process is public and the labour is unassignable - you can persuade contributors, never task them. That constraint shapes every gate in the flow we're about to walk, and it's also the thing that DOESN'T hold inside our own companies. Worth planting that now; the room will pick at it later. Django: now narrow to the example. The point is age and philosophy. Django is old enough that its process has been stress-tested, and opinionated enough that it has to decide what does and doesn't belong in core. The published design philosophy means "does this fit Django?" is a question the intake process can answer rather than a matter of taste.

Who maintains Django

Governance structure & roles

  • Steering Council — elected; rules on direction and significant changes
  • Mergers — appointed by the Steering Council; the only ones who commit to main
  • Fellows — paid contributors who triage, review and keep the lights on
  • Wider community
The key point: "open source" here doesn't mean "anyone can push". It means the process is public. Note the Fellows - Django solved the maintainer-burnout problem by paying people to do the unglamorous work. Worth flagging that as a deliberate design decision, not an accident.

Over to the Room

  • Are there aspects of Django’s process worth borrowing for our own work — and anything from how we work that could improve Django’s contribution process?
  • Has anyone got experience contributing to other open source projects? How does it compare?
  • What is AI doing to open source?
This is the slide the whole walkthrough was setting up - leave most of the session for it. Start with the first question while the flow is fresh, then widen out to other projects, and finish on AI. Useful contrasts if the room needs a nudge: CPython's PEPs and Steering Council (Django borrowed the model), FastAPI's single-maintainer model, projects that live or die on one volunteer. On AI: floods of low-effort generated PRs and security reports vs. AI helping newcomers get over the first-contribution hump. The goal isn't to resolve anything, it's to surface how differently people in the room handle it.
